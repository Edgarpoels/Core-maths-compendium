Graphs tell a story, and linear models

3.11a▾

On a graph, steeper means a faster rate of change, flat means no change, and a jump (discontinuity) means a sudden change such as a new price band. A linear model $y = mx + c$ has gradient $m$ (the rate of change, with units) and $y$-intercept $c$ (the starting or fixed value). Always say where the model stops being sensible.

Graph sketching and quadratic and cubic models

3.11b▾

When sketching, mark the intercepts with the axes, the turning points and the overall shape. A quadratic is a $\cup$ or $\cap$ shape; its turning point is halfway between its roots. A cubic has an S-shape. Quadratic models describe things like the height of a thrown ball or the area of a rectangle.

Solving equations graphically

3.11c▾

The solutions of $f(x) = g(x)$ are the $x$-coordinates where the graphs of $y = f(x)$ and $y = g(x)$ intersect. The roots of $f(x) = 0$ are where the curve crosses the $x$-axis. To solve a new equation, rearrange it so that one side is the equation of a curve you already have, then draw the line for the other side.

Worked example

A taxi charges £3 plus £2 per kilometre. Write down the cost $C$ for $d$ km, and find the cost of a 7.5 km journey.

The fixed charge is £3 (the $y$-intercept) and the rate is £2 per km (the gradient), so $C = 3 + 2d$.

For $d = 7.5$: $C = 3 + 2 \times 7.5 = 3 + 15 = $ £18.

Exam tip. Always give the gradient its units ('£2 per km') and say what the intercept means in the story ('the fixed charge'). When asked to comment on a model, say where it stops making sense — for example a negative distance or cost is impossible.
